Havana, Cuba

Jul 2016 · 2016-07-11

It's a cliche but arriving to Havana really is like stepping back in time. Although our airport taxi was new (thanks to some laws that didn't exist in 2010 when Elle first visited), we had 1950s American classic cars around us, decaying colonial buildings whizzing by and a conspicuous lack of advertising (bar some pro revolution messages and images of Che Guevara). The very different landscape on the way into central Havana is surprising and fascinating and feels more like a film set.

After settling into our casa particular—a typical Cuban family home—we set out on a mission to find money and water, not always as easy as it sounds in a country that doesn't trade with its closest neighbour. We managed to get the money without too much hassle, the service and air conditioning in the bank was among the best in city, if not Latin America. Next up we tackled the chaotic, yet amazingly low on stock supermarket and with four bottles of water in hand went in search of the end of the queue. After snaking around tens of people waiting haphazardly with food resting on freezers and on shelves, we took our place and eventually paid at the 1930s-esque register. Just visiting the supermarket was an experience.

Another interesting experience was visiting the Museum of the Revolution, based in the old presidential palace (no long required since the revolution). While the museum at face value is fairly unexciting, with a hard to follow collection of items that vaguely link to the revolution (shoes once warn by Castro, or a mug that Che once drank from), what is interesting is the very different version of events described. Events like the Cuban Missile Crisis and Bay of Pigs are described with reference to the "infidel" Americans, and the barbarian ways of the previous government. The museum's temporary exhibition was a series of photographs named Gracias por todo, Fidel, or thank you for everything Fidel, which definitely gives the impression of a country that is preparing for the death of the revolutionary leader, and a government that is worried about what that could cause to follow.

After dinner we made a quick trip to the famous malecon to watch the sunset, meeting some local characters along the way and watching tens of fisherman fish from the exact same spot...all with little success.

Next up was Adam's birthday so we decided to head to Playas del Este, some beautiful Caribbean beaches 25km outside Havana. We boarded the new Chinese-branded coach in the Central Park and arrived at the fairly undeveloped beach a short time later. We hired ourselves an umbrella and sun beds, procured some beers and pizza and lazed the day away in front of the warm, turquoise water. It is easy to forget in the chaos and searing heat of Havana city that you are on a Caribbean island.

Feeling the need to fulfil our tourist obligations we headed into Havana Vieja, the old city. The area is very pretty but a lot more done up, and a lot more touristy than where we were staying in Havana Centro. We saw the main sites on a self guided walking tour, including the cathedral, main plazas and forts. The heat was extreme so we wrapped things up with a well deserved drink at a microbrewery and some shopping at the souvenir market.

We had planned to spend Saturday in the tobacco fields of Vinales, but the government run tour company had other ideas and left us waiting outside a hotel in Havana. After a long queue and some quintessentially poor customer service we managed to sort out a replacement tour for Sunday and moved on to make the most of the day. Embarrassingly, making the most of the day entailed us boarding an open top tourist bus on the loop through the other major suburbs of Vedado and into Miramar (where Fidel Castro apparently lives). We did the full loop and jumped off again at the Plaza of the Revolution. The plaza is a massive flat space resembling a car park overlooked by the famous murals of the national heroes Che Guevara and Camilo Cienfuegos. Impressive yes, but also hot and a little sparse.

Our second attempt at the Vinales tour was more successful, they picked us up for a start. The tour was a little how you would imagine North Korea does its tours—every single bus stops at the same series of tourist spots, all of course government owned and run, and delivers the same spiel. The first stop was a rum factory that was not in operation and largely served as a point to sell rum and cigars to tourists. This was followed by a stop to view a strange painting of dinosaurs on the side of a hill and a very short boat trip around a cave in the side of hill. Thankfully, they saved the best to last and we visited a family tobacco farm. Here we learnt how they farm, harvest and dry tobacco, followed by a demonstration of hand rolling cigars. It was a great insight into life in Cuba, and interesting to learn that the government takes around 90% of the tobacco produced by each farm, leaving the farmers with very little to live on.

We decided to spend our last day in Cuba at the Partagas cigar factory in Havana, it's the factory that makes the famous Cohiba branded cigars, famously smoked by Fidel himself. The factory itself isn't huge and there wasn't a lot to see but it was an interesting insight into the Cuban workplace. Again it felt like a bit of time warp—rows of people sat at small wooden desks, methodically working to roll, press and measure cigars. Apparently the workers get 45 minutes of news read to them each morning and then a variety of music played throughout the day, and they all receive five cigars per day...which we think were later being pedalled by workers in the factory lobby.

We spent the rest of the day trying to procure some bottled water, as it seemed the island had (literally) run dry. We eventually found some, but it certainly makes you realise how difficult life in Cuba can be, and how easy we have it. After some more wandering around Havana and the malecon, we were ready to head back to Mexico City, where food and water were a little easier to come by...and the customer service seems like a dream.
