Panama and Costa Rica

Jun 2016 · 2016-06-11

Panama

Having reached the top of the continent we farewelled South America and quickly landed in Panama City. Honestly we weren't terribly impressed with Panama on the whole so apologies for the negativity. The city itself looks a lot like Miami, very shiny and filled with American chains and expensive everything. It was also unbelievably humid and the very wet start to the 9 month-long rainy season...these facts added to our disapproval.

Deciding to make the best of it we headed out to what is Panama's most famous attraction—the canal—at Miraflores locks. We spent a few hours mesmerised by the series of locks that lift and drop boats across the narrow passage between the Pacific and Atlantic oceans. We waited patiently as a boat packed with wind turbines floated from one side to the other through three sets of locks. It's an impressive beast, and there's soon to be more with the new third channel opening this month. Trying to get out of there proved to be difficult but we eventually Ubered our way back to the city—you've got one thing Panama, perhaps some public transportation to it would make sense?!

Next up we tackled Panama's history in the historical centre and the old Panama City ruins, making Uber our best friend. Both areas were very nice and extremely similar to Havana, crumbling old buildings and grand plazas. The historical centre has a million restoration projects taking place and will look pretty spectacular when it's all done up. Sadly though, one old guy we met pointed from the old centre back toward the skyscrapers and said "and that's Miami", thus confirming our previous thoughts on the city.

The ruins of Old Panama City sit about 10km south of the new city, and was the original settlement used by the Spanish to transport goods to and from the Pacific Ocean. That was until pirates attacked and destroyed the city in 1671, and the city was subsequently moved to where it still is today. We explored the town until daily afternoon rains hit at lunchtime, as they do every day during the wet season.

Having seen about all that Panama had to offer, we sought a way up to continue our march toward Mexico. This led us to bus to a fairly crappy but common halfway point to Costa Rica called David. We'd expected to pass through quickly and carry on to San Jose the next morning...this wasn't to be. Spooked by other traveller stories of demanding immigration requirements at the Costa Rica border, we decided to stay an extra night and put together some documentation proving we'd be leaving Costa Rica within 30 days. We built an itinerary, it rained a lot and we boarded the bus to San Jose.

Then came a really comical border crossing to Costa Rica. First we stopped and waited for Panamanian authorities to check our bags on the way out. Given the whole bus was now in a small room our bags didn't fit on the benches, so we were allowed to go without opening our stuff. Then we arrived to Costa Rica where the immigration lady barely looked up, didn't say a word, stamped our passports and let us in...so much for the entry requirements and that day we spent building evidence for! Talking to other travellers, how much documentation you're required to show is more down to luck (and the immigration officers' mood) than any rules or regulations. Next up we headed into a cage and waited with our fellow bus passengers for customs to check our gear. After 20 minutes a guy dressed in tshirt and jeans entered, took everyone's declaration paper and let everyone back on this bus, again no need to open the luggage. And with that we were in and headed to San Jose, our first of many Central American border crossing had gone well, and quickly.

Costa Rica


San Jose is another unremarkable big Central American city. We were using it as a base point to take day tours and then ultimately bus to the Costa Rican cloud forest jungle at Monteverde. Unfortunately Elle got really sick and spent most of her time in bed, so really only saw two blocks of San Jose. Adam managed to get out to a tour of Poás volcano and surrounds. Poás, just an hour or so from San Jose, has the largest crater of an active volcano in the world at about 1 mile wide. Luckily the activity is currently low, allowing you to hike right up to the edge of the crater from where you can see a pool of sulphuric acid, with clouds of gas billowing out.

We chalked up our time in San Jose as a rest stop and kept moving north to the cloud forest with great hopes of seeing sloths and toucans.

A fairly short and easy bus ride later we arrived in Monteverde, a very green town but also an exceptionally wet one. We took a night walk and day tour in the cloud forest and while we didn't see either of our target species we did see a quetzal, tarantula, viper and several huge walking stick insects...and several types of bird which many fellow travellers were excited about but which we didn't feel that pumped about. It's hard to get excited about birds.

We couldn't help but feel a little disappointed by our experience in Costa Rica, the jungle felt more like a reserve or someone's very large and expensive backyard and we hadn't seen what we'd set out to. (This was probably our own fault for making the wrong choice of stops rather than any reflection on what Costa Rica has to offer). Finding a sloth became like a gambling addiction, having invested so much time so far there was no leaving without seeing one, and determined to get our fix we decided to head further north to Nicaragua.
