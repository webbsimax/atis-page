Iguazu Falls, Argentina/Brazil

Apr 2016 · 2016-04-05

After Easter we said goodbye to Buenos Aires and hello to Iguazu falls—one of the biggest sets of waterfalls in the world (taller than Niagara Falls and twice as wide) and a new natural wonder of the world. We flew into Puerto Iguazu, Argentina, a small town that is a gateway to the falls and sits on the border with Brazil and Paraguay. Not really much to report from the town itself, aside from the extreme humidity and red soil.

On our first evening we headed to a viewpoint just outside of the town where Rivers Paraná and Iguazu meet. From this point in Argentina you can see Brazil and Paraguay just over the rivers.

We spent our first day in the falls wandering the platform boardwalks to view the falls from all angles. The first glimpse across the length of the falls was pretty amazing, water as far as we could see. The boardwalks stretch across the river and around the corner with falls everywhere, with one walk on top—overlooking the falls—and the other below—where the spray drenches anyone brave enough to get close. We ended the first day by getting the train up to see the Devil's Throat from above—a huge mass of water where the falls meet on the border of Argentina and Brazil and crash over the edge of the rocks, spraying the viewing platforms with a fine mist. It was great to see and feel the power of the waterfalls and get covered in water and butterflies!

One of the major hazards of Iguazu is the coatis, raccoon like animals that roam the park. While they may look cute and cuddly, the moment you try to eat anything hordes of them appear from all angles. The park is full of graphic pictures of what could happen if they get to close, and many stories of coatis stealing bags if they contain food.

We returned for a second day (entry was half price when you went back), which saw us searching for tapirs and toucans on a jungle trail within the Argentinean park that leads to a small waterfall. It was a nice walk and a nice waterfall deep in the jungle, which would've been more impressive had it not been for the falls we'd spent the previous day marvelling over! We saw some monkeys, a big rat thing (the largest rodent in the world) and some lizards...nothing too exciting. We spent the afternoon at the Devil's Throat again, which was a lot misty-er.

After a few days on the Argentinean side of Iguazu, it was time to make the 15 minute taxi ride over to Foz Do Iguaçu, Brazil to see the other side of the falls. Crossing the border was simple, our taxi driver did the hard work and we didn't even have to leave the taxi to clear immigration into Brazil.

The next day, we took a fairly intense and full public bus to the falls, with a driver who seemed intent on making everyone fall over, or dislocating an arm if you were one of the lucky few with something to hold on to. Once there we boarded a far more sedate open top double decker bus through the Brazilian park. This side was smaller and has less to offer as most of the falls fall on the Argentinian side, but the boardwalk led to some pretty amazing panoramic views of the other side of the river. We saw the falls from afar and then walked along to see the Devil's Throat close up. Here we felt the spray of the falls and were lucky that the weather was good and rainbows were forming in front of the falls and birds soaring above. Very mystical. The Brazilian side gives a much better sense of just how big the falls are—they're more than 2.5km wide!

That afternoon we headed to see some man made water action just up the river to the Itaipu hydroelectric dam, sitting on the Paraná River that borders Brazil and Paraguay, half owned by each country. The power plant produces approximately 80% of Paraguay's electricity, and 17% of Brazil's, enough to make it the second largest hydroelectric dam in the world in terms of maximum power, and the largest in total generated over a year.

We joined a tour of the dam, which included a bus trip to a few viewing points to see the huge dam (over 7km long) and its spillway, down which flows 40 times the flow of the Iguazu falls. We were then taken inside the dam wall to see the massive structure up close, where staff use bicycles to get around rooms kilometres long, and over 100m high as well as the control centre and generators up close.

This part of the trip was over quickly and on Sunday we left Foz via Sao Paolo to Rio de Janeiro. Time to get our (non-existent) Portuguese on...it sounds nothing like Spanish!
