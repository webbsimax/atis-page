Berlin, Germany

Nov 2016 · 2016-11-04

On arrival in Berlin, we quickly set about on a quintessential Berlin experience, heading to the oldest purveyor of currywurst in town for dinner. The communist era dish of sausage, chips, tomato sauce and curry powder was delicious, and refreshingly cheap after a few weeks in Scandinavia.

The following morning we set out to see Berlin's most famous artefact, the wall that divided the city for 28 years. On the way to see one of the remaining sections, we came across a market selling everything from food, to cameras, to artwork, and plenty of musicians and street performers. We wondered the stalls for a while, picking up some souvenirs and a compulsory cup of glühwein (it was nearly midday...).

We made our way to see the rest of the wall, a large section of which has been preserved next to a small museum featuring stories of families suddenly split when the wall was built, and attempts—both successful and unsuccessful—to cross the wall.

We finished the day back at the market, where a karaoke session was just starting. About 2000 people soon gathered and joined in, with various levels of talent and sobriety. We watched, not having had quite enough glühwein to join in. It was great to see the alternative, and seemingly un-Germanic side of Berlin.

On our second day we took a walking tour of the city, covering many of the historically important sites. We walked between the Brandenburg Tor, the Holocaust memorial, Checkpoint Charlie (now a huge tourist trap with apparently very little resemblance of anything historic), the WWII Luftwaffe HQ, the SS and Gestapo HQ, and the site of Hitler's bunker (now a unremarkable car park with no evidence of what lay underneath it).

After our tour we took the metro towards another remaining stretch of the Berlin Wall, known as the east side gallery. The 1.3km stretch has been painted with murals on what was once the eastern side of the divide as a memorial to freedom.

Our final day in the German capital was spent exploring a few museums. The first was on the site of the former SS and Gestapo headquarters—called the Topography of Terrors. The museum chronicles the history of the Nazi police forces, their horrific practices, and the fate of many the senior officers following the fall of the third reich. We then moved on to the Holocaust memorial, which is made up of more than 2000 concrete blocks of different heights scattered across an undulating plaza. Underneath is a small memorial with numerous personal stories of those killed in death camps.

Our short stay in Berlin was soon over, and before we knew it we were on a train, heading for Amsterdam.
